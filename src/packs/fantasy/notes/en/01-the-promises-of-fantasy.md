# The promises of fantasy

A reader who opens a fantasy novel wants to be taken somewhere else, and to trust that the place will hold. This is what you promise, and what the reader notices when you break it.

- **Wonder.** At least once per part, the reader should see something they have never seen: a city built inside a waterfall, magic that sounds like glass cracking. Wonder is the heart of the genre. _Without it the world feels like ours with other names._
- **A world that holds together.** What was true in chapter three is true in chapter thirty. If fire cannot be tamed at the start, it cannot suddenly obey at the end. _If the rules slip, the reader stops trusting._
- **Magic that costs.** The reader wants to feel that every spell takes something. Magic without a price becomes a shortcut, and shortcuts kill tension. _Without price and limit, any problem can be solved with a spell._
- **Stakes on two scales.** The world is at stake, but what makes the reader care is personal: a sister, a promise, a home. _If only the world is at stake, the stakes turn abstract._
- **A main character who grows.** The person standing there at the end is not the one we met at the start. The journey through the world is also a journey inward. _Otherwise the journey is a sightseeing tour._
- **A what if that carries the book.** Every fantasy rests on an idea: what if memories could be sold, what if the gods died when no one prayed to them any more. The idea should be felt throughout the book, in the magic, in the struggle for power and in the main character's own choice. _If it isn't felt, it lives only on the cover._
- **An ending that settles the book's question.** The conflict this book has built up is resolved in this book, even in a series. The series arc can live on, but the reader should close the book feeling that something has been decided. _Otherwise the reader is unwilling to buy the next part._

**Breaking one on purpose.** Readers forgive a broken convention when they can tell it was a choice. Break one promise at a time: magic without a price can carry a fairy tale if you are knowingly writing one. Keep the rest rock solid.

**The first chapter is a contract.** The opening pages promise what kind of book this is: how much magic, how dark, how big. If the dragons arrive in chapter twelve, let the reader sense them in chapter one. A promise never made can never be kept.

**Epic or urban, and more.** Epic fantasy promises a whole world, a long road and stakes that reach across kingdoms. Urban fantasy promises our own city with something hidden beneath it, and a main character who lives in both. The promises above hold for both, but the weight falls differently. The guide Epic and urban fantasy covers the differences, and the guide Subgenres takes grimdark, cozy and more.

**Try this:** write your book's what if in a single sentence. If you can't, that is the first thing to solve.
