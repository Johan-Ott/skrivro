# The world in the scene

The iceberg principle: you know ten times more about the world than the reader ever sees. What shows should feel like the tip of something vast.

**The opening**

- **Start with a person, not a map.** The first page shows someone who wants something, in a concrete situation. The world appears in what they see, touch and take for granted.
- **Something familiar, something new.** Bread and rain the reader recognizes. A baker who charges in dreams they don't. That balance gives the reader a foothold.
- **The particular over the general.** Not a market, but stalls selling salted eel and weather in bottles. One exact detail carries more world than a paragraph of description.
- **No more than one new name per page at first.** Every invented word costs the reader effort. Write the emperor before you write Vaskar the Third of Orrun.

**Exposition in scene**

- **Let conflict carry the knowledge.** Two people arguing over a law explain it better than any narrator.
- **Nobody explains what both already know.** "As you know, brother, we have been at war for a hundred years" is a warning sign. Use a newcomer, a misunderstanding or someone who is wrong instead.
- **Raise the question first, answer it later.** Information the reader has been longing for feels like a reward. The same information too early feels like homework.
- **Use the character's attitude.** How they feel about magic, a people or a god says more than facts. "No honest soul goes down to the river after dark" is worldbuilding and character at once.
- **The world through the character's eyes.** They know only what they have learned and believe what they have been told, even when it is wrong. Let prejudice and misunderstanding be part of the picture, so the reader gets to discover the truth themselves.

**Names**

- Give each people its own sounds and patterns in names, so the reader can hear where someone comes from.
- Avoid names that look alike. Kael, Kaelen and Kaela in the same book is two too many.
- Go easy on apostrophes and unusual letters. A name the reader can't pronounce in their head slides past.

**Chapter endings**

- A chapter may end quietly, as long as the reader is already carrying an open question.

**Pacing**

- Long journeys shrink to a sentence if nothing happens on the road. Give every journey an event that changes something.
- After a big battle the reader needs a quieter scene where the consequences land.
- Tag scenes with the Wonder label. If several chapters pass between them, the book has lost its heart for a while.
