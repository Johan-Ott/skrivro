# Epic and urban fantasy

Both are fantasy, but they promise different things and demand different craft. Decide which book you are writing, or how you are blending them, before you plot.

**Epic fantasy**

- **Scale is the promise.** Kingdoms, wars, ancient powers. But the scale must be anchored in a few people whose fates the reader follows up close.
- **Several viewpoints, in moderation.** More viewpoint characters give breadth but thin out each thread. Give every viewpoint its own arc and its own reason to exist, and ask whether two of them could be one.
- **History as pressure.** The old war, the broken oath, the fallen house. History should act on the present, as debt, as revenge, as a claim to the throne. Otherwise it is just scenery.
- **The journey changes things.** Every new place should cost something or give something: an ally, a lie exposed, a rule revealed. A landscape that is merely passed through belongs in your notes.
- **The series.** If this is book one, resolve its own question and let the larger question grow. Readers buy the next book because this one kept its promise.

**Urban fantasy**

- **The city is a character.** The streets, the weather, the night buses and the way people talk should be exact enough to make the hidden world believable. Write about a city you know, or research until you know which bus runs last at night.
- **The secret has a mechanism.** Why doesn't everyone know? Magic that makes people forget, a law among the initiated, or plain unwillingness to see. Decide too why phone cameras, news feeds and the authorities haven't exposed anything.
- **Two lives that chafe.** The main character has a job, a family or rent to pay, and the hidden world demands time and lies. The conflict between the two lives is the engine.
- **Factions.** The hidden world is rarely united. Let groups with their own aims, old pacts and territories bring politics and tension, and put the main character between them.
- **Voice.** The genre is often carried by a close, personal narrating voice, frequently with dry humour in the middle of danger. Decide early how close the reader gets and how the main character sounds.
- **The plot as a puzzle.** Many urban fantasy novels are built like an investigation: a body, a disappearance, a threat to the secret. Lay out the clues as carefully as in a crime novel.

**Blends.** Portal fantasy takes someone from our world into another and thrives on the contrast. Low fantasy keeps magic rare and dangerous in an otherwise everyday world. Whatever blend you choose, the same holds: the reader should understand early which rules apply.

**Try this:** write down three things your book promises that only this subgenre can give. Then check that the first chapter hints at all three.
