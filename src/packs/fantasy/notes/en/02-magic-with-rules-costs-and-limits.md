# Magic with rules, costs and limits

The reader doesn't need to understand how your magic works. They need to understand what it can't do. Tension lives in the limits.

**The rule of thumb:** magic can solve a problem only to the extent the reader already understands it. Magic that causes problems can be as mysterious as you like.

- **The rules.** Decide what magic does, how it is triggered and who can use it. Example: a threadspinner can bind together two things that have touched, but never something the spinner hasn't touched themselves.
- **The cost.** Every use takes something: strength, memories, years of life, a relationship, standing. Example: each binding leaves the spinner's hands colder, until they feel nothing at all. The cost bites hardest when it takes exactly what the main character values most.
- **The limit.** What can never be done, however much you pay. Raising the dead, changing the past, bending a will. What magic can't do is more interesting than what it can, and the limit is your best source of drama.
- **Deepen before you add.** One power whose consequences you have thought through gives more than three new ones. Ask what the magic you already have could be used for, by the wrong person and for the wrong ends, before you invent another kind.
- **The consequences for society.** If magic exists, who profits from it and who fears it? Are there laws, guilds, persecution? How has it changed war, medicine, trade and faith? Magic shapes the world, not just the battles.
- **Magic and theme.** The strongest magic says something about the book. If the book is about letting go, make the magic demand that you give something away. Then every spell is also a moral choice.
- **Show before you explain.** Let the reader see magic used and the cost paid before anyone explains it. A spinner who can no longer hold a cup of tea says more than a page of theory.
- **Stick to the rules.** Write them in your magic system note and reread them before you write the final battle. If the solution needs a new rule, plant it early.

**Soft or hard magic.** Soft magic is mysterious and creates wonder and danger, but must not resolve the main conflict, because the reader can't see the solution coming. Hard magic has clear rules and can carry the solution, because the reader can anticipate it and enjoy watching the main character use the rules cleverly. Most books mix the two: hard rules for what the main character does, and a soft mystery around the great unknown. What matters is that you know which one you are writing in each scene.

**Try this:** write down three things your magic can never do. Then find a scene where one of them stands in the main character's way.
