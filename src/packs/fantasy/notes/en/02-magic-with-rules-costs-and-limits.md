# Magic with rules, costs and limits

The reader doesn't need to understand how your magic works. They need to understand what it can't do. Tension lives in the limits.

**The rule of thumb:** magic that solves a problem must already be understood by the reader. Magic that causes problems can be as mysterious as you like.

- **The rules.** Decide what magic does, how it is triggered and who can use it. Example: a threadspinner can bind together two things that have touched, but never something the spinner hasn't touched themselves.
- **The cost.** Every use takes something: strength, memories, years of life, a relationship. Example: each binding leaves the spinner's hands colder, until they feel nothing at all.
- **The limit.** What can never be done, however much you pay. Raising the dead, changing the past, bending a will. The limit is your best source of drama.
- **The consequences for society.** If magic exists, who profits from it and who fears it? Are there laws, guilds, persecution? Magic shapes the world, not just the battles.
- **Show before you explain.** Let the reader see magic used and the cost paid before anyone explains it. A spinner who can no longer hold a cup of tea says more than a page of theory.
- **Stick to the rules.** Write them in your magic system note and reread them before you write the final battle. If the solution needs a new rule, plant it early.

**Soft or hard magic.** Soft magic is mysterious and creates wonder, but must not resolve the main conflict. Hard magic has clear rules and can carry the solution, because the reader can see it coming. Most books mix the two: hard rules for what the main character does, and a soft mystery around the great unknown.

**Try this:** write down three things your magic can never do. Then find a scene where one of them stands in the main character's way.
