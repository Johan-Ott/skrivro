# Names, language and maps

Names, words and maps are the first thing the reader meets of your world. They can draw the reader in, or push them away before the chapter is over.

**Names that can be read.**

- **The reader should be able to pronounce it in their head.** A name that is a string of consonants stops the reading. Test by reading aloud.
- **Different in different places.** A people, a region or a class gets a sound pattern: the same endings, the same kind of syllables. Then the world feels coherent without your explaining.
- **Keep the names apart.** Two characters who start with the same letter get mixed up. Give the main characters different first letters, lengths and rhythms.
- **Let names mean something.** A people who name their children after weather or trades reveals a culture in one line. If you know the meaning, no one else needs to.
- **Titles and houses.** Keep name, title and family apart, so the reader knows who is who.

**Words and language.**

- **A few words, well chosen.** Three or four invented words that recur are enough. More makes reading heavy.
- **Meaning through context.** Do not explain in a paragraph. Let the word appear in a scene where you can tell what it is.
- **Language that belongs to a culture.** How a greeting, an insult and a thank-you sound says more about a people than a history book.
- **Translation.** If the characters speak another language than the reader reads, decide how it shows, for example plain prose, with a few words left in the original.
- **Inflection.** Decide plural and definite forms for your words, and write them down in the style sheet.

**Maps.**

- **Draw it before you write the journeys.** Distances and terrain decide how long a journey takes and what obstacles it has. Make a simple map and measure.
- **Let the map have a history.** Borders follow mountains, rivers and wars. A border as straight as a ruler says someone drew it.
- **Journeys take time.** Count days, rest, food and weather. The reader notices if a journey goes too fast.
- **The map in the book.** A map at the start promises a journey. Include only the places that matter, and avoid spoilers.
- **What the map doesn't know.** Let characters have different pictures of the world. Someone who never left the village doesn't know what lies beyond the valley.

**Try this:** read aloud the ten names the reader will meet first and see which get mixed up. Change the two that sound alike.
