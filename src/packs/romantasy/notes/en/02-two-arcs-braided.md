# Two arcs braided

A romantasy has two arcs that must hold each other up: the love story and the world. Ask the same two questions of every chapter:

**What happens between them?** Resistance, being forced to work together, respect, trust, closeness, betrayal, choice.

**What happens in the world?** The quest, the threat, the magic waking, the power closing in on them.

The strongest chapters are those where the answers are tied together: a turn in the world forces a turn between them, or the other way round. Give the two arcs roughly equal room, in the scenes and in the ending. If the world takes nearly everything, you have a fantasy with a love story in the margins; if love takes nearly everything, the world becomes a backdrop.

- **Let the world make the obstacles.** They are enemies because their kingdoms are at war, not because they misread each other. She hunts witches, and he carries the magic she swore to wipe out.
- **Let the magic touch the relationship.** A bond that shares pain, a curse only the other can break, a power that grows with closeness. Then love and plot cannot be pulled apart.
- **The bond does not replace the choice.** Fated mates, soulmates, a magic that pulls them together: much-loved tropes, but the bond must not do the work for them. Let them choose each other despite the bond, or against it. The love should make sense without the magic.
- **Slow burn in steps.** Every step closer should cost something or change something. Enemies become reluctant allies, then something like friends, then something more dangerous.
- **Two voices.** With dual POV the reader knows more than either of them. Decide what the reader learns: if they know the secret, you get suspense, because they are waiting for it to blow; if they know nothing, you get surprise. Both work, but choose on purpose. Give each voice its own vocabulary, its own fears and its own goal.
- **Tropes with intent.** Enemies to lovers, only one bed, arranged marriage, the dangerous protector, a bargain with a hidden price. Pick the trope that forces out your lead's inner conflict, deliver what it promises and give it a turn the reader did not see coming.
- **The lie and the truth.** Both carry a false belief about themselves or about love. The crisis in the world drags it into the light, and love makes it possible to let go.
- **Power between them.** When one is a prince and the other a prisoner, let the gap show, and let it shrink. Respect grows when the more powerful one chooses not to use that power.
- **The people around them.** A small circle of friends, siblings or comrades in arms gives the couple someone to protect and someone who tells the truth. Found family is a trope of its own that many readers look for.

The midpoint is the clearest meeting of the arcs: the first kiss or night comes as a truth about the world changes what they are fighting for. The ending is the second meeting: the grand gesture is part of the deciding battle, not something that happens afterwards.

**Rough markers.** The meeting within the first tenth of the book, the bargain around a quarter, the midpoint at half, the betrayal around three quarters, and after the battle at least one full chapter where the couple get to be together. These are rules of thumb, not laws, but when something drifts far from them, readers tend to feel it.
