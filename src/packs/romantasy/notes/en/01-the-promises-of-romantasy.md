# The promises of romantasy

A reader who picks up romantasy is buying two promises at once: a love story and a fantasy. Break either one and the book feels like a betrayal, however good the rest is.

- **Love carries the book.** The relationship is one of two main plots, not a subplot. Take it out and the fantasy plot should collapse. _If love becomes a side plot, it is fantasy with a romance, not romantasy._
- **A happy ending, or a promise.** The couple choose each other: for ever, or for now with a promise that holds until the next book in the series. A couple parted for good breaks the genre. _If the couple is parted for good the genre breaks, and the next book has to pay back._
- **A world that costs.** The magic has rules and a price, and the powers around them create the real obstacles to love. _If magic is free, it solves problems that love should solve._
- **A fantasy that also resolves.** The war may go on in the next book, but this book's threat gets its battle and its answer. Fantasy readers want to be fed too. _If the threat hangs over into the next book, the fantasy reader feels cheated._
- **Heat that is earned.** Tension builds in steps: resistance, being forced together, respect, closeness, almost. The reader should wait, but never wait for nothing. _If the heat comes too early the tension ends, and if it never comes the reader waits for nothing._
- **Two people with a will of their own.** Both want something, make their own choices and have something to lose. Neither is just a prize for the other. _If one of them is a prize, the falling in love collapses._
- **Consent on the page.** Even in a dark, dangerous world it is clear that both want this. Danger and power can charge the space between them, but closeness is a choice. _If danger removes the choice, the closeness is no choice._
- **Stakes on two levels.** What is at stake in the world is also at stake between them. When the realm shakes, so does the bond. _If the world's crisis doesn't touch the couple, it becomes two books in one._

**Breaking one on purpose.** An open ending for the series can work if the couple have chosen each other and the next book is in debt to the reader. A dark ending can work if the book promised darkness from page one and the reader was warned. Break one promise at a time and pay back with something that weighs more.

**Choose your kind of romantasy.** Heat, tone and the size of the world shift. The guide Subgenres goes through fae, academy, court, cozy and the others.

**Promise early.** By the first chapters the reader should know what kind of book this is: how dark, how steamy, how epic. If the first scene shows an execution in the square and a look between two enemies in the crowd, you have promised both danger and heat. Keep that promise, in the same tone, all the way.

**The heat level.** Decide how much of the sensual is shown, from closed door to open, and stay there. A sudden leap in either direction feels like a different book. Many readers choose books by heat level, or spice level as it is often called, so state it openly in the book's description.

**Content warnings.** Dark romantasy often holds war, torture, coercion or abuse in the world around the couple. A short list of content warnings at the front of the book is common in the genre. It spoils no surprise, and it lets the right readers dare to pick the book up.
