# The promises of historical fiction

A reader of historical fiction wants to travel in time, but inside a story that pulls them along, not a lesson. This is what you promise.

- **An age that feels real.** The reader should smell the tar, feel the cold in the cottage and know what a pair of boots cost. Details convince, not dates. _Otherwise the book could happen at any time._
- **People who think like their time.** The characters hold their era's beliefs, fears and certainties. They are not modern people in costume. _Otherwise you remove the strangeness the reader came for._
- **What is known is right.** Dates, places and the outcome of real events are correct, or you account for where you changed them. Readers of the genre often know a lot, and they notice. _Knowledgeable readers stop trusting the book at the first mistake._
- **History matters to the characters.** The great events are not a backdrop. They break into lives and force choices. _If it is only a backdrop, the book has no reason to happen then._
- **Suspense despite the known ending.** The reader may know how the war ended. The suspense lies in what happens to these particular people. _Otherwise the reader stops caring once they know how it goes._
- **The research stays out of sight.** It shows in everything being right, not in being explained. _If the research lectures, the reader gets a lesson in the middle of a scene._
- **An honest author's note.** The reader wants to know what is true and what is invented. A short note is both a courtesy and a protection for you. _Without it the reader wonders what is true, and suspects you of hiding it._

**An example:** A farm maid in a famine year wants to marry, but without a dowry she cannot. The famine is real and historical. The maid, the farm and her choices are invented. The reader learns more about the famine from her counting the flour in the bin than from any statistics.

**Breaking one on purpose.** Change a known event if you say so in the author's note and the change serves the story. Let a character think like today if they have to pay for it. A deliberately modern language can work if it is a choice that carries the whole book, not carelessness. Break one promise at a time. The guide Subgenres goes through the adventure novel, romance, the mystery and the others.

Use these promises as a yardstick when you plan: which chapter shows the age through daily life, where does history break in, and where does the reader learn what is true?
