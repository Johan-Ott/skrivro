# The promises of historical fiction

A reader of historical fiction wants to travel in time, but inside a story that pulls them along, not a lesson. This is what you promise.

- **An age that feels real.** The reader should smell the tar, feel the cold in the cottage and know what a pair of boots cost. Details convince, not dates.
- **People who think like their time.** The characters hold their era's beliefs, fears and certainties. They are not modern people in costume.
- **What is known is right.** Dates, places and the outcome of real events are correct, or you account for where you changed them. Readers of the genre often know a lot, and they notice.
- **History matters to the characters.** The great events are not a backdrop. They break into lives and force choices.
- **Suspense despite the known ending.** The reader may know how the war ended. The suspense lies in what happens to these particular people.
- **The research stays out of sight.** It shows in everything being right, not in being explained.
- **An honest author's note.** The reader wants to know what is true and what is invented. A short note is both a courtesy and a protection for you.

**An example:** A farm maid in a famine year wants to marry, but without a dowry she cannot. The famine is real and historical. The maid, the farm and her choices are invented. The reader learns more about the famine from her counting the flour in the bin than from any statistics.

Use these promises as a yardstick when you plan: which chapter shows the age through daily life, where does history break in, and where does the reader learn what is true?
