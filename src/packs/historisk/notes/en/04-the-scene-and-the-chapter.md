# The scene and the chapter

A historical novel wins or loses its reader in the first pages, and in how the age is woven into every scene.

**The opening.** Start with a person who wants something, in the middle of a concrete situation. Not with a date, the weather or a summary of the political situation. A boy running through the town with a letter he must not drop shows more of the age than a page of background.

**Orient the reader fast.** Within the first page the reader should roughly know where and when. Do it with a detail or two, a church, a coin, a custom. A heading with place and year can help, but it does not replace the details in the prose.

**One detail per sense.** Pick a sound, a smell and something to touch in each new setting. Three well chosen details are enough. More turns into an inventory.

**Background in small doses.** If the reader must grasp a historical situation, let it come through quarrels, rumours and misunderstandings between characters. One character explaining something to another who already knows it is a sign you are lecturing.

**Point of view.** The character knows only what they could know. A soldier in a battle does not know how the battle is going. A merchant knows prices, not affairs of state. The narrow view makes history vivid and creates suspense.

**Pace.** Historical novels can bear a calmer pace, but every scene must still move something: a relationship, a decision, a threat. Skip time when nothing happens, and mark the jumps clearly.

**The chapter ending.** End where the personal and the historical meet. A message arrives. A door closes for good. Someone makes a decision the age will punish.

**Example:** She has counted the flour three times and arrived at the same answer. That evening she hears the priest will read something from the pulpit on Sunday, about emigration.

**Label the scenes.** Put Real event and Liberty taken on your scenes, so you can pull them up in Contents when you write the author's note. Put Period detail where a detail carries the age. Filter on it and see whether a chapter lacks anchoring, or another is overloaded.
