# Common pitfalls and revision

**The pitfalls**

- **The moral.** The book exists to teach something, and the reader can tell. Let the protagonist get it wrong and find out for themselves. The reader draws the conclusion without being told.
- **The rescuing adult.** A parent, a police officer or a teacher solves the problem in the last chapter. That takes the victory away from the protagonist and from the reader.
- **An adult voice in a young head.** The twelve-year-old thinks "I realised my fear was really about loss". Write what they do and feel, not what a therapist would say.
- **Childhood memories instead of now.** The book is about how it was for you, back in your day. Young readers want to be in their own present, or in a world that feels just as alive.
- **The wrong length.** A 30,000-word chapter book or a 120,000-word YA novel. Stay close to the age band's range.
- **The empty friend.** The friend only exists to agree. Give them goals of their own that sometimes clash.
- **Darkness without light.** A heavy subject with no hope can leave a young reader alone with it. Offer a way out, even a small one.
- **A slow start.** Three chapters of background before anything happens. Start where it starts.

**Revision, in three passes**

Pass 1, the structure:

- Go through each scene and ask: who acts? If it isn't the protagonist, could it be?
- Read the hard scenes as a parent of the reader and as the reader. Are you true to both?

Pass 2, the scenes:

- Read the first page of every chapter. Does something happen there already?
- Read the last paragraph of every chapter. Is there a hook?

Pass 3, the lines:

- Cut explanations after lines of dialogue. Trust that the reader got it.
- Replace words a reader of that age won't know, unless context explains them. In a chapter book, tag them Tricky word.

Finally:

- Have a reader of the right age read it, and ask where they stopped wanting to read. Not what they thought of it.
