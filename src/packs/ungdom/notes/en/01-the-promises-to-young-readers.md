# The promises to young readers

Young readers put a book down without a twinge of guilt. These are the promises you must keep to make them read on.

- **The protagonist solves their own problem.** Adults may help, get in the way or be absent, but the decisive step is taken by the child or teenager. Otherwise the book feels like a lesson.
- **It starts straight away.** The first page shows someone who wants something, and something that is already wrong. Background can wait.
- **Now, not then.** The reader should be in the middle of what is happening, feeling it in the protagonist's body. Not an adult remembering what it was like to be twelve.
- **The reader is taken seriously.** Young people's problems are huge to them: a friend who lets them down, a parent moving out, a rumour in class. Write them that big.
- **Chapters that pull.** Short chapters that end on a question, a surprise or a decision. The reader should think "just one more".
- **Hope at the end.** Not everything has to be solved, but the protagonist has grown and the world is liveable. The younger the reader, the safer the ending.
- **Hard themes, handled with respect.** Death, divorce, bullying, anxiety and bodies belong in books for young readers. Tell it truthfully, without preaching or wallowing, and let the reader's age decide how close you go.
- **Humour and warmth.** Even a serious book needs moments where the reader can laugh or breathe out.

Try this: describe the book's problem in one sentence the reader might say themselves. "My best friend has started hanging out with the others." If that sentence doesn't move a twelve-year-old, neither will the book.
