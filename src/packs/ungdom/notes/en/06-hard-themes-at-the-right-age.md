# Hard themes at the right age

Young readers already meet death, divorce, bullying and anxiety. A book can be the place where they watch someone get through it. The question is rarely whether you may write about it, but how close you go.

**Chapter book, ages 6 to 9**

- Fear, a pet that dies, a grandparent who is ill, arguments at home and being left out are all fine to write about.
- Keep the hardship in what happens, not in the details. Let a safe adult be close by, even when the child solves the problem.
- The ending is safe. What broke doesn't have to be mended, but the child isn't alone with it.

**Middle grade, ages 9 to 12**

- Death, illness, divorce, bullying, poverty, fleeing a home country, a changing body and a first crush all belong here.
- Violence and sexuality are hinted at, not shown.
- Somewhere in the story there is an adult who can be trusted, even if they don't solve it.

**Young adult, ages 13 and up**

- Sex, alcohol and drugs, mental illness, self-harm, abuse, violence and racism can all be portrayed. The closer you go, the more important it is that the consequences show too.
- A book for younger teens, roughly 12 to 15, is usually more restrained than one for older YA readers. Decide which you are writing.

**How to write it**

- **From the inside.** Show what it does to the protagonist, not facts about the subject. The reader should recognise themselves, not be informed.
- **No preaching, no wallowing.** The hard part is there for the story's sake, not to shock or to teach.
- **Suicide and self-harm.** Never describe methods in detail, and never let death look like a solution or something beautiful. Show that help exists and that things can get better. These are the guidelines that exist for how the media portray suicide, and publishers follow them.
- **Support afterwards.** A short afterword can tell the reader where to turn: the school counsellor, a doctor or a support line for young people. Check which ones exist where the book is published.
- **Other people's experiences.** If you write about something you haven't lived yourself, such as a disability, a culture or a sexuality, have someone who has read the manuscript. Publishers often call it a sensitivity read.
- **Swearing.** In YA it can be there wherever the character would use it. For younger readers, find the character's own expression instead.
- **The people who buy the book.** Chapter books and middle grade are mostly bought by parents, teachers and librarians. That is no reason to avoid what is hard, but you should know why it is there.

Tag the passages with Difficult theme, and read them in one go before you send the manuscript on: once as the reader and once as a parent of the reader.
