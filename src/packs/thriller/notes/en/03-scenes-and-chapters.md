# Scenes and chapters

In a thriller the chapter is the engine. The reader should never find a good place to put the book down.

- **The opening.** Start in motion, in a scene that shows what the main character stands to lose and casts a first shadow of threat. No pages of backstory before something happens.
- **Short chapters.** Often 1,500 to 3,000 words. A chapter is a single question raised and almost answered.
- **Every scene has a goal, opposition and an outcome.** The main character wants something, something stands in the way, and the result is worse than they hoped. She gets the key, but the alarm goes off.
- **Chapter endings that pull.** Vary the kind: a threat (a voice behind her), a revelation (a name she recognizes), a decision (she takes the gun from the drawer), a question (how did he know where she lived?). Physical danger alone gets repetitive.
- **Set pieces.** Plan three to five big sequences in places that look nothing alike: a roof in a storm, a stalled train, a packed christening. Each one should turn the plot, not just be dangerous, and the place should offer both obstacles and chances.
- **Breathers with tension.** After a big scene, a short moment lets the main character process, understand and make a new decision. The clock keeps ticking here too. Label them Breather and keep them short.
- **Point of view.** Close third person or first person gives the most feeling. Keep the villain's point of view short and rare: show intent, not identity if it is meant to stay hidden. An unreliable narrator may conceal but not cheat: every sentence should read two ways once the truth is out.
- **Sentences follow the pulse.** Short sentences in action, longer ones in dread and waiting. Concrete sensory detail in danger: the click of a lock, cold air from a window.
- **Cut away.** With several viewpoints you can switch right when something is at stake, and make the reader wait a chapter for the answer.
