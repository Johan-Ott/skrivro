# The clock and the stakes

A thriller runs on two forces: time running out, and the price of failure going up. Plan both before you write.

**Make the clock concrete.** At midnight, before the plane lands, when the trial opens on Monday. Remind the reader often: a phone showing the time, a call from the villain, a dated headline.

**Shrink the clock.** Somewhere after the midpoint, time gets shorter than expected. The villain moves the date forward, a route closes, a witness disappears. What the reader was counting on no longer holds.

**The stakes ladder.** Let the stakes climb in three steps:

- **External:** life, freedom, money. If the bomb goes off, two hundred people die.
- **Personal:** someone the main character loves. Her daughter is on the passenger list.
- **Inner:** who they are. To stop it, she has to do exactly what she swore never to do again.

**The villain drives the plot.** Write the villain's plan as its own timeline, in The villain's plan. Every time the main character gets closer, the villain answers with a move. A villain who acts between scenes makes the world feel alive and dangerous.

**Yes, but. No, and.** Rarely let an attempt succeed cleanly. If it works, it creates a new problem. If it fails, things are worse than before.

**Let the reader know more.** If the reader knows there is a bomb under the table, a calm conversation at that table becomes unbearable. Short scenes from the villain's point of view can give the reader that knowledge without giving everything away.

**Plant the twists.** A twist no one could have seen coming feels cheap. Lay down two or three seeds early, so the reader can look back and see the answer was there.

**Kinds of twist.** Vary them, so the reader does not learn to wait for the same thing.

- **Who:** the ally works for the villain, or the victim is the one who planned it all.
- **Why:** the villain wanted something other than the main character thought, and the plan was never about what looked like the target.
- **When and where:** what the threat was about has already happened, or is happening somewhere else right now.
- **What they know about themselves:** the main character is wrong about their own past, or the narrator has held something back all along.

Test every twist: does what came before make more sense in hindsight, or none at all? Only the first is a real twist.

**In Skrivro.** Put the labels Time pressure and Stakes rise on scenes and filter in Contents. Are there long stretches with neither? That is where the pace sags.
