# The psychological thriller

Here the threat does not live in another city but in the same house. The question is not only whether the main character makes it in time, but whether they can trust anyone, even themselves.

**The threat from someone close.** A partner, a sister, a neighbour, a new friend who knows a little too much. Give that person real warmth at the start. The reader must understand why the main character trusted them, or they just look naive.

**The closed setting.** A home, a workplace, a holiday resort. The place should feel both safe and shut in: doors that lock, but also lock from the inside. Everyday things carry the threat: a cup of tea, a phone in the wrong place, a key on another hook.

**Paranoia and manipulation on the page.** Show it in small, concrete steps. Someone insists she never said that. A text has been deleted. Friends have heard a different version. Let the reader doubt at the same pace as the main character, and give every incident two possible explanations: one harmless, one frightening.

**The unreliable narrator, played fair.** The narrator may leave things out, misread and lie to themselves, but the text must not lie to the reader. Plant the signs: a gap in time, a reaction that is too strong, something others say that does not fit the narrator's picture. At the reveal the reader should be able to go back and see that every sentence held.

**The clock when the danger lives in the house.** There is rarely a bomb, so the clock is a date or an event drawing near: the wedding, the move, the birth, the day the papers are signed. The clock can also be the body. Lack of sleep, medication or a pregnancy makes the main character weaker with every chapter. Calling the police is no way out if no one would believe them; say why on the page.

**The showdown.** It often happens at home, and the victory is that the main character finally trusts their own picture of what is happening.
