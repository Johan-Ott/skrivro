# The protagonist who acts

The hunted one is easy to write as someone who only runs. Then the book becomes a string of events that happen to them, and the reader worries but does not cheer. What makes a thriller work is that the protagonist chooses, and every choice costs.

**Three kinds of choice.**

- **The choice under pressure.** There are two ways and both are bad. They choose, and the result shows in the next scene.
- **The choice to go toward the danger.** They turn back, call, open the door. It hands them the initiative and makes the reader lean forward.
- **The choice between two loyalties.** The mission or their own, the truth or a friend. That is where the inner stakes show.

**Reactive or active?** Go through the first half and count: how many scenes begin with the protagonist wanting something, and how many with something happening to them? Both are needed, but after about a quarter the weight should shift.

**Mistakes are good.** Let them guess wrong, trust the wrong person, give up too soon. The reader forgives mistakes that come out of something they want, but not mistakes that are only there so the plot can move on.

**Competence should have a price.** What they can do can also hurt them. A former police officer sees a trap but cannot trust their own memory of what happened last time.

**The private weakness drives.** What do they never want to do again? Save it for the last act, when they have the least time left.

**Try this:** write one line per scene in the first half: "They want ... but ...". If a scene lacks a goal and an obstacle, it is a scene where something merely happens.
