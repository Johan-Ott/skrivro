# Credibility without lecturing

A reader forgives a lot in a thriller, but not what is plainly wrong. A single wrong detail about something the reader knows makes them doubt the rest. At the same time it is not your job to teach a profession.

**Research is for you, not for the page.** Read until you can answer what happens next. Then write only what the protagonist notices, needs or is frightened by. Ten percent of what you know is enough.

**Three questions for every detail.**

- Does it change what someone does in the scene? Then it is needed.
- Is it what makes the scene feel real? Then it is needed, briefly.
- Is it only proof that you have done the reading? Cut it.

**Let a person carry the knowledge.** Instead of an explanation: a helper who is irritated by a question, a protagonist who makes a mistake and is corrected, a rule that someone breaks and pays for.

**Where it is easy to slip.**

- **Police and the justice system.** Routines differ between countries. Ask a police officer or read up on how it works where your book is set, and decide deliberately where you simplify.
- **Weapons and violence.** Consequences and the body matter more than model names. An injury should still be there in the next chapter.
- **Technology and surveillance.** Technology dates and must not do what the protagonist should do. Decide what it can do in your world and stick to it.
- **Distance and time.** Count. How long does the trip take, what time is it, how much has the protagonist slept? A mistake in the timeline stops the clock.
- **A profession or setting you do not know.** Have someone who does read those scenes.

**When you decide to cheat.** Do it deliberately and in small ways. The reader will buy a code cracked in fifteen minutes if the scene needs it, but not that it gives exactly what is needed, just in time, at no cost.

**In Skrivro.** Collect what you have checked in Sources, with how certain it is and in which chapters it is used. Then you know what rests on what when a factual question comes.
