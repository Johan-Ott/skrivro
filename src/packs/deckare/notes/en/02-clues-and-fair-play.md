# Clues and fair play

A clue should be seen without shining. At the end the reader should think "it was right there", not "I was never told that".

**Plan backwards.** First write What really happened, in Clues. Then list every trace the culprit left behind. Those are your clues. Everything else is scenery or red herrings.

**Three kinds of clue.** Mix them, so the reader cannot learn where they hide.

- **Physical:** an object, a mark, something missing. The coffee cup on the rack has been washed, though the victim never washed up.
- **Testimony:** something said in passing. The neighbor mentions how quiet the evening was, though the dog always barks at visitors.
- **Behavior:** a reaction that does not fit. The sister never asks how he died.

**Hide in plain sight.**

- Put the clue in the middle of a list, or in a sentence about something else.
- Place it just before a dramatic moment that pulls the eye elsewhere.
- Let another character misread it, out loud. Readers like to take the first explanation offered.
- Give the culprit an honest reason to be at the scene, so their presence raises no questions.

**Three times.** The decisive clue often appears three times: once in passing, once when it raises a question, and once when it is explained.

**Red herrings must be true.** What the text says is accurate, it just points the wrong way. The suspect lies about where she was, but to hide an affair, not a murder. Every red herring needs its own explanation, resolved before the end.

**Inside the detective's head.** If the story follows the detective's thoughts, it cannot hold back what they have already worked out. Delay the insight instead: they notice the detail, get interrupted, and only understand later.

**In Skrivro.** Put the label Clue or Red herring on scenes and filter in Contents. Are the clues spread out, or bunched in the last third? Fill in When on the chapters and sort by Chronological order to see whether alibis hold or break where they should.
