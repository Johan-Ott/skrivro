# The promises of crime fiction

Anyone who opens a mystery has made a deal with you. Break it and the book feels like cheating, however good the prose. After each promise comes the price of breaking it.

- **A crime that asks a question.** The crime is discovered in the first chapter, or within the first tenth of the book. Who and why are asked early and kept alive all the way. _If the crime comes late, the reader wonders what the book is about._
- **The culprit is there from the start.** The reader has met them, ideally within the first third and in a role that seems harmless. _A culprit who walks on in the last act is a betrayal, not a surprise._
- **Fair play.** Every clue the solution rests on is shown to the reader before the detective draws the conclusion. The reader should be able to solve it, and ideally fail to. _If a clue is hidden from the reader, the book feels like cheating._
- **The detective solves the case.** Through work, wit and nerve. Not through luck, a last-minute confession or a newcomer who suddenly knows everything. _Otherwise the detective becomes a spectator in their own book._
- **A motive that makes sense.** The reader need not forgive, but must understand. Money, fear, love, shame and revenge drive almost every mystery. What makes yours your own is the circumstances. _An empty motive makes the reveal flat._
- **More than one possible answer.** At least three suspects who could have done it. Readers want to guess, change their minds and guess again. _With only one real suspect there is no puzzle._
- **Order restored, at a price.** The truth comes out, but the case leaves marks on the detective and on those left behind. _Without a price the ending is soon forgotten._
- **A setting with a voice.** A mill town in November, a dental practice, a sailing club. The place shapes the crime, the suspects and what can be hidden. _If the book could happen anywhere, it becomes interchangeable._

**Breaking one on purpose.** Readers forgive a broken convention when they can tell it was a choice. Break one promise at a time and keep the rest rock solid. Give something back that weighs more than what you took: a mystery without a full solution can work if the question that remains is what the book is really about.

**Choose your kind of mystery.** The promises hold for all of them, but the weight shifts. Decide which one you are writing before you choose your detective, because it settles their authority, their method and how much police work the book needs. The guide Subgenres goes through the classic puzzle, the cozy mystery, the police procedural and the others.

**Test your manuscript:** can you point to the page where the reader first meets the culprit, and the page where the decisive clue first appears? If the meeting falls in the first third and the clue before the midpoint, you are keeping the most important promises.
