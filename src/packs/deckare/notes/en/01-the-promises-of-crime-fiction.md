# The promises of crime fiction

Anyone who opens a mystery has made a deal with you. Break it and the book feels like cheating, however good the prose.

- **A crime that asks a question.** The crime is discovered in the first chapter, or within the first tenth of the book. Who and why are asked early and kept alive all the way.
- **The culprit is there from the start.** The reader has met them, ideally within the first third and in a role that seems harmless. A culprit who walks on in the last act is a betrayal.
- **Fair play.** Every clue the solution rests on is shown to the reader before the detective draws the conclusion. The reader should be able to solve it, and ideally fail to.
- **The detective solves the case.** Through work, wit and nerve. Not through luck, a last-minute confession or a newcomer who suddenly knows everything.
- **A motive that makes sense.** The reader need not forgive, but must understand. Money, fear, love, shame and revenge drive almost every mystery. What makes yours your own is the circumstances.
- **More than one possible answer.** At least three suspects who could have done it. Readers want to guess, change their minds and guess again.
- **Order restored, at a price.** The truth comes out, but the case leaves marks on the detective and on those left behind.
- **A setting with a voice.** A mill town in November, a dental practice, a sailing club. The place shapes the crime, the suspects and what can be hidden.

**Choose your kind of mystery.** The promises hold for all of them, but the weight shifts. Decide which one you are writing before you choose your detective, because it settles their authority, their method and how much police work the book needs.

- **The classic puzzle:** a closed circle, a riddle and a solution that can be worked out. Fair play matters most, and the reader competes with the detective.
- **The police procedural:** a team, real working methods and often several viewpoints. Credible police work and the relationships in the squad matter as much as the puzzle.
- **The dark mystery:** the crime as a mirror of a place, a society or the detective. The cost and the mood matter most, but the puzzle still has to hold.
- **The cosy mystery:** an amateur, a small community and the violence kept offstage. Warmth and humour are part of the promise, and no reader wants to see a child die.

**Test your manuscript:** can you point to the page where the reader first meets the culprit, and the page where the decisive clue first appears? If the meeting falls in the first third and the clue before the midpoint, you are keeping the most important promises.
