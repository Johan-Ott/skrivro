# Scenes and chapters

A mystery is read with a question at the back of the mind. Every scene should either sharpen the question or change the answer.

- **The first page.** Start close to the crime: the discovery, the minutes before, or the first person to sense something is wrong. A short prologue from the victim's point of view works if it does not give away who.
- **Every scene moves the case.** A new fact, a new suspect, a lie that cracks or a theory that falls. If a scene shows the detective's private life, it must also do one of these, or mirror the case.
- **The interview as a scene.** Both sides want something: one to find out, the other to hide, protect or stall. Let the detective win one thing and lose another. The caretaker admits he lent out the key, but not to whom.
- **End chapters on a question.** A find, a call cut off, a name turning up where it should not. She turned the photo over. On the back was her own name.
- **Pace by alternation.** Alternate thinking and movement. After two chapters of talk, something needs to happen: a discovery, a threat, someone going missing.
- **A clue a chapter.** Rule of thumb for the middle: every chapter holds at least one clue, red herring or exposed lie.
- **Point of view.** One viewpoint, the detective's, makes fair play easiest. Several viewpoints add suspense, but every voice must conceal honestly. Never let the culprit think in plain words that they are innocent.
- **Details that carry weight.** Describe with concrete, memorable things and let the clues hide among them. Five items on a desk make an excellent hiding place.
- **The reveal as a scene.** Let the pieces fall into place with the culprit in the room and something at stake. The explanation can come after, but keep it short.
