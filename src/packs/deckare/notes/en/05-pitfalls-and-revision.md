# Pitfalls and revision

**Common pitfalls**

- **The solution out of nowhere.** A clue the reader never saw feels like cheating. Plant it earlier, in passing.
- **The culprit stands out.** They are the only one with a strong motive, or the story noticeably avoids them. Give other suspects equally good reasons, and let the culprit appear just often enough.
- **The detective gets lucky.** A witness who happens to walk in with the answer steals the win. Let work and insight decide it.
- **The lecture at the end.** Ten pages of explanation kill the tension. Let the pieces fall into place in a scene, and explain only what the reader has not already grasped.
- **The sagging middle.** Interview after interview with nothing changing. A second crime, a false solution or a threat to the detective keeps the pace up.
- **Too many suspects.** Three to five the reader remembers beat ten they mix up. Give each one a distinct trait and a lie of their own.
- **Procedure as filler.** Routine work that raises no questions can be summed up in a sentence.
- **The motive in the final chapter.** The reader needs to have seen the seed of the motive earlier, even if they did not understand it.

**Revision, in this order**

- Read only the scenes labelled Clue. Can the puzzle be solved from them?
- Read the book as the culprit. Do they do anything that only makes sense if they are innocent? Cut it or explain it.
- Follow each red herring to its resolution. Is any thread left hanging?
- Give the manuscript to a test reader and ask at three points who they think did it. If everyone guesses right early, hide better. If no one understands the solution even afterwards, show more clearly.
- Check every chapter ending: does it end on a question?
- Trim the reveal to what is truly needed.
