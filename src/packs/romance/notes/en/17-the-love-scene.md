# The love scene: closeness, heat and consent

The most anticipated scene in a romance is also the easiest to get wrong. It should not be a display, it should be a turning point: something changes between the two, and the reader should feel it.

**It is all about what changes.** Ask what the scene does to the relationship. Before it they are one way, after it another. Do they dare to say something honest? Does one show a side they have hidden? Does it make the wall crack, or make something close become too close? If you can't answer, the scene can be cut or shortened.

**Heat level, again.** You have decided it (see The promises of romance), and it applies to the scene: closed door, half-open or open. Keep to it. A reader who chose the book by its level should not be surprised. Ask what a closed door shows: often a glance, a hand, the last sentence before the door shuts is enough.

**Consent as part of the tension.** Consent is not a question asked and ticked off. It is part of the pull: glances, questions, a "do you want to?" as charged as a kiss, a "stay" that means more than it sounds. Show that both want it, with the body and with words. That is what makes the scene sexy, not what removes the sexiness. A hesitation that is respected is a scene too: it builds trust, and the next yes is stronger.

**Body and feeling together.** The physical without the inner becomes mechanical, and the inner without the physical becomes vague. Alternate: a hand, and what it stirs; a word, and what it costs. Use the senses, not only sight: sound, breath, warmth, scent.

**The people, not the positions.** Let the scene show who they are: one who always leads and now lets go, the other who always helps and now asks. That is where the reader recognizes themselves.

**The language.** Decide your tone: playful, serious, tender or hungry. Avoid words that pull the reader out of the scene by sounding clinical or silly. Read aloud to find where it chafes.

**Humor and uncertainty.** A laugh in the middle, a clumsiness, a "wait, I have to ..." makes the characters human. Perfect is not sexy, honest is.

**Afterwards.** The scene's power often lies in what happens next: the silence, a new way of seeing each other, the fear that comes back. Show what has changed, and how the wall is built up or torn down.

**Content note.** If the book has darker or more explicit content than the reader expects, say so in the description. It protects the reader and you.

**In Skrivro.** Mark scenes with Spark, Tension or Closeness and look at the curve through the book. Is there a stretch with no closeness, or one where it comes too early?
