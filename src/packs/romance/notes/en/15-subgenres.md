# Subgenres

The promises of romance hold for all of them, but each subgenre has its own weight. Feel-good and romantic comedy have their own guide, Feel-good and romantic comedy. Romantasy and paranormal romance have their own pack, Romantasy. Here are the others.

**Contemporary romance**

- The promise: a couple in our time, where jobs, rent and phones both hinder and help.
- Set-up: daily life is the obstacle. The conflict comes from the people, not the plot.
- Pitfall: an obstacle one short talk would solve. Give them a real reason.

**Historical romance**

- The promise: love in another time, with its rules, clothes and classes.
- Set-up: the norms of the age are the opposition. A marriage, a rank, a reputation.
- Pitfall: modern thinking in costume. Let the couple want something the age makes hard.

**Small-town romance**

- The promise: a small place, a community and a couple who belong there.
- Set-up: supporting cast, yearly festivals and a place that becomes part of the plot.
- Pitfall: sugared scenery. Give the town a problem the couple must take a side on.

**Romantic suspense**

- The promise: love and danger in one book, with a mystery or a threat.
- Set-up: the danger forces the couple close, and trust is what they risk.
- Pitfall: forgetting the love in the chase. Both threads must be resolved.

**Workplace romance**

- The promise: the office, kitchen or team as the arena. Power, reputation and rules are the obstacle.
- Set-up: they depend on each other, and so they cannot fall.
- Sports romance is the same arena with a team and a season as the clock.
- Pitfall: a power gap nobody takes seriously. Show that both choose.

**Second-chapter romance (mature)**

- The promise: love after divorce, loss or a life that turned into something else.
- Set-up: they have a past, children, habits and scars that need room.
- Pitfall: making age an excuse. Let it give depth, not just weariness.
  **Dark romance**
- The promise: a dark desire, often with power, coercion or violence in the relationship. It plays with the line of consent, but the book knows it does and says so openly, with a content warning.
- Set-up: the threat is part of the pull, and the road there should be clear to the reader.
- Pitfall: violence romanticised without the book noticing. Give it consequences.

**Ask yourself:**

- Which subgenre is mine, and which am I drifting into by accident?
- What is the obstacle here that the others don't have?
- What level of heat have I promised, and do I keep it all the way through?
