# Tropes and promises

A trope is a pattern the reader recognizes and longs for: enemies to lovers, a fake relationship, only one bed. It is a promise, not a mold.

**The rule.** Every trope has a rule that keeps the couple in place: "we're pretending", "we hate each other", "just one night". Write the rule down, and what would make it crack.

**The payoff.** The payoffs are the moments when the rule cracks, and the biggest is when it falls completely. Write them as scenes of their own, with time and pressure, because the reader wants to see the crack, not hear about it afterwards. Do not pay off too early, and do pay off.

**What makes it fresh.** Change one thing, not the trope: who holds the power, what the rule costs, where it is set or who cracks first. Recognize the form and swap the filling.

**Let it force the inner.** A good rule puts the couple's wounds under pressure. If they can be together without facing their fears, the trope is wrong for them.

**A trope is not a person.** The grump is a trope. A person with a reason to be grumpy is a character. Give both.

**The trope sells the book.** Readers search by trope, and bookshops and social media sort by them. Put the trope in the first lines of the blurb, in the words readers type into the search box, such as "enemies to lovers", "fake dating", "grumpy sunshine" or "slow burn". Write them out in the text, not as a tag line at the end. Name one, two at most: one trope carries the structure, the rest is seasoning. And promise only what the book pays off, because a trope in the blurb that never cracks in the book loses you the reader.
