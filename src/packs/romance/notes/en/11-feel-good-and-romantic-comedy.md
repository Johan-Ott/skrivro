# Feel-good and romantic comedy

Feel-good and romantic comedy share a great deal with romance, but they promise something more: that the book will feel good to be inside. That takes craft, not just cosy settings.

**The promises.** Feel-good and romantic comedy promise what romance promises, and also:

- **Warmth.** A place and people the reader would like to move in with.
- **Laughter.** The humour comes from the characters and the situations, not from making them stupid.
- **Hope.** Grief, divorce and hard things are allowed, but the ending leaves the reader lighter than they began.
- **A new life.** The lead's own journey, starting over, finding home or daring, matters as much as the love story. The ending must be happy for them, and almost always for the couple too.

**The craft**

- **Two stories in one.** In feel-good, the lead's new life carries as much as the love story: a business to build, a family to make peace with, a place to belong to. Let the two stories help and hinder each other.
- **Romantic comedy is a romance first.** There the love story is the main plot, and the humour is the way it is told. The jokes should move the couple closer or further apart, not just fill space.
- **The comedy comes from the character.** Someone with a firm rule, a pride or a bad plan is funny on their own. Put them where the rule collides with reality. "She had promised herself never to cook for a man again. Now she stood in a stranger's kitchen with a leg of lamb in the oven and twenty guests on their way."
- **Let it get worse.** A comic scene gets funnier with every step that goes wrong. Let each attempt to save the situation make it worse, and let the collapse reveal something true about the character.
- **Warmth without sugar.** The community becomes believable when the people are also difficult: the neighbour who complains, the mother who meddles. Give each side character a quirk and a problem of their own.
- **Room for the serious.** Grief, divorce, loneliness and illness are common in feel-good. Take them seriously. The humour should lighten, not belittle, and the heavy makes the light stronger.
- **The place as a promise.** The reader wants to move there. Give the place seasons, smells, an everyday life and a threat. The island or the café the reader has met many times before needs something that exists only in your book.
- **The setback must sting.** Even in the warmest book, the reader should doubt for a while that it will work. The more the place and the couple have to lose, the warmer the ending.
- **A pace that breathes.** Feel-good can carry calmer chapters than a pure romance, but each chapter must still move something: in the love story, in the new life or for the place.

**The ending.** The feel-good ending is a celebration, a new season or an everyday life that has turned out better than the plan. Let the reader linger in the good for a while, ideally with the side characters gathered, before the last page.
