# The promises of romance

A romance has a contract with its reader. The reader knows how it ends, and that is exactly why they read: to see how it gets there.

- **The love story is the main plot.** Everything else in the book, the job, the family, the town, is there to pressure or help the relationship. _If the job or a mystery takes over, the couple becomes decoration._
- **A happy ending.** The couple choose each other. Happily ever after (HEA) or happy for now (HFN) with a clear future. Without it, it is not a romance, however beautifully it is written. _If it ends unhappy it is a love story but not a romance, and the reader should know before they buy._
- **Emotional justice.** Whoever hurts the other truly apologizes, and whoever dares is rewarded. The reader should feel the couple have earned their ending. _If the one who hurt gets away with it, the ending feels unfair._
- **Two whole people.** Both have a life, a goal and a wound of their own. Neither exists only to rescue or reward the other. _If one person is only a reward, the falling in love goes thin._
- **Chemistry you can see.** The reader should feel the pull in the scene, in looks, lines and bodies, not just be told it is there. _Without chemistry in the scene, the love is only a claim._
- **Consent and respect.** It is clear that both want this. A no is respected, and whoever crosses a line faces the consequences. _If it is missing, the reader puts the book down, and rightly. Dark romance plays with the line, but the book knows it does and says so openly, with a content warning._
- **The tone holds.** If the first chapter promises warmth and humor, the book should not suddenly turn brutal. _If tone or heat jumps, it feels like a different book._

A clear promise on page one can look like this: "Linnea had three rules for her new life on the island. No men, no seagulls in the kitchen, no rushing. By nine in the morning she had broken two." The reader knows at once what the book promises: humor, a place and a love story.

**Breaking one on purpose.** The happy ending, HEA or HFN, is the genre's line and the one promise you never break: break it and the book is something else, and it should say so on the cover. Happy for now still counts, and a relationship that has to wait can work in a series if the next book pays back. Otherwise break one promise at a time and give the reader something that weighs more.

**Choose your kind of romance.** The weight shifts between contemporary, historical, small-town and the rest. The guide Subgenres goes through them, and Feel-good and romantic comedy has its own guide.

**The heat level.** Decide early how much of the sensual is shown, from closed door to open, and keep that level all the way through. Feel-good usually sits at closed or half-open door; romance can sit anywhere. Many readers choose books by heat level, or spice level as it is often called, so state it openly in the book's description.

**The answer to the question.** The reader knows it ends happily. The tension lies in how these two will get there, with the wounds and the reasons you have given them. The more impossible it looks halfway through, the stronger the ending.
