# The promises of romance

A romance has a contract with its reader. The reader knows how it ends, and that is exactly why they read: to see how it gets there.

- **The love story is the main plot.** Everything else in the book, the job, the family, the town, is there to pressure or help the relationship.
- **A happy ending.** The couple choose each other. Happily ever after, or happy for now with a clear future. Without it, it is not a romance, however beautifully it is written.
- **Emotional justice.** Whoever hurts the other truly apologises, and whoever dares is rewarded. The reader should feel the couple have earned their ending.
- **Two whole people.** Both have a life, a goal and a wound of their own. Neither exists only to rescue or reward the other.
- **Chemistry you can see.** The reader should feel the pull in the scene, in looks, lines and bodies, not just be told it is there.
- **Consent and respect.** It is clear that both want this. A no is respected, and whoever crosses a line faces the consequences.
- **The tone holds.** If the first chapter promises warmth and humour, the book should not suddenly turn brutal.

**Feel-good and romantic comedy** promise more:

- **Warmth.** A place and people the reader would like to move in with.
- **Laughter.** The humour comes from the characters and the situations, not from making them stupid.
- **Hope.** Grief, divorce and hard things are allowed, but the ending leaves the reader lighter than they began.

A clear promise on page one can look like this: "Linnea had three rules for her new life on the island. No men, no seagulls in the kitchen, no rushing. By nine in the morning she had broken two." The reader knows at once what the book promises: humour, a place and a love story.

**The heat level.** Decide early how much of the sensual is shown, from closed door to open, and keep that level all the way through.
