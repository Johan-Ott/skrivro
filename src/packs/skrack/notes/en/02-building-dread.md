# Building dread

A shock is a moment. Dread is what happens between the moments, and dread is what carries a horror novel.

**Dread before shock.** Let the reader know something is coming before it comes. A woman hears scratching under the floor three nights running. On the fourth night it is silent, and that is the worst of all.

**The unseen is scariest.** Show the threat late and in pieces: a shadow, a sound, a track, what it does to people. The reader's imagination fills the gaps better than your description can. When you finally show it, show one detail that is wrong rather than the whole thing.

**Rules make the threat real.** Decide what it wants, what it can do, what it cannot do and what draws it out. Show the rules through events, not explanations. The fact that it only comes when someone is alone in a room tells the reader more than a lecture on demons.

**Isolation.** Cut off help step by step: nobody believes you, the phone loses signal, the friend goes home, the snow closes the road. Isolation can be inner too, like shame or a secret you dare not share.

**The everyday makes the uncanny.** Horror lives on contrast. The more ordinary life you show, the coffee, the kids nagging, a broken dishwasher, the more it jars when something is wrong.

**What is the fear about?** Ask what the threat stands for. A body that changes can be about illness. A house that will not let go can be about inheritance and guilt. Let the main character's private fear and the threat mirror each other, so the confrontation becomes personal.

**Escalation.** Draw a staircase: each step should be closer, more personal or less reversible than the one before. If two events in a row are equally bad, swap them or cut one.

**Respite.** Fear wears out. Give the reader quiet scenes where they can grow attached to the people. The respite is what makes the next step on the staircase land.
