# The promises of horror

A horror reader wants to be frightened, but in a way that feels earned. These are the promises you make, and the reader notices at once when you break one.

- **Unease arrives early.** The first chapter needs something that is off, even if it is only a detail. A door left ajar that was shut a minute ago is enough. _If it comes late, the reader loses patience._
- **It gets worse.** Each part of the book is more dangerous than the last. A threat that stands still stops being frightening. _If the threat stands still, it becomes familiar._
- **The threat has rules.** The reader does not have to understand them, but must sense that they exist. A threat that can do anything is not scary, only random. _Without rules anything is possible, so nothing is frightening._
- **It costs.** Someone the reader cares about can be hurt or killed. Without risk there is no fear. _Without someone to lose there is no fear._
- **The main character cannot just leave.** There is a reason to stay, or the way out is closed. The reader should never have to wonder why they do not simply move. _Otherwise the reader wonders why they don't move out, and loses faith._
- **The fear means something.** Under the supernatural or the monstrous lies a human fear: losing a child, losing your mind, not being enough, being alone. That is what lingers. _Without it the monster is only a monster._
- **The ending echoes.** Horror may end dark, open or with a fragile victory. Either way it must feel like the result of what came before, and ideally leave one last chill. _If the ending takes it all back, as a dream or a hoax, the reader feels cheated._

**Breaking one on purpose.** An ending without victory suits a book that promised darkness. A threat never explained can work if the horror lies in not understanding. More blood does not mean more horror, so show the aftermath. Break one promise at a time and pay back with a last unease that grows from what has happened.

**Choose your kind of horror.** The guide What kind of horror covers where the threat comes from, and the guide Subgenres covers folk horror, gothic and the others.

**An example:** A father moves with his daughter to a house by a lake. The supernatural element is something that lures children out onto the ice. What he is really afraid of is failing to protect her now that her mother is dead. Every scene on the ice presses on exactly that fear.

Reread these promises when you plan the structure, and ask which chapter keeps each one.
