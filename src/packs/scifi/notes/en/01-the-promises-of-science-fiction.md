# The promises of science fiction

Above all, science fiction promises one thing: to take an idea seriously and follow it all the way.

- **A clear what if.** The book rests on one change to our world: what if sleep could be downloaded, what if a ship took five generations to arrive. After a few chapters, the reader should be able to state the idea themselves.
- **Consequences, not just gadgets.** The technology isn't the point. The point is what it does to people, power, love and work.
- **Logic that holds.** The world follows its own rules. If the speed of light is a limit at the start, it is one at the end, or breaking it is the story.
- **Strange and familiar at once.** The world should be strange enough to stir curiosity and familiar enough to say something about ours. The genre does its thinking in the gap between the two.
- **Wonder.** At some point the reader should catch their breath at the scale of it: an ocean under the ice, a city that thinks, a century passing during a single voyage.
- **People at the centre.** The big idea is felt through someone with something to lose. Without that person, the book is an essay.
- **Credibility over precision.** The reader doesn't need the equations, but should believe you have them. One confident detail beats a long explanation.
- **A question that lingers.** The best of the genre leaves the reader thinking about our own time.

**Subgenres make different promises.** Space adventure promises speed, scale and large conflicts. Hard science fiction promises that the science holds and that the solution can be worked out. Near future promises recognition and unease. Dystopia promises a system to fight. First contact promises a meeting with something truly alien. The story after the catastrophe promises a fallen world, and the question of what is worth rebuilding. Decide which promise your first page makes, and keep it.

**Try this:** write your book's what if in one sentence, and its most important consequence for ordinary people in one more.
