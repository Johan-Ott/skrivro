# The world in the scene

The iceberg principle: you know far more about the technology, the history and the society than the reader ever sees. What shows should hint at the rest.

**The opening**

- **Start with someone doing something.** A pilot changing a filter, a girl queueing for water. The world shows in what they do out of habit.
- **Let the strange be ordinary to the character.** They don't marvel at the gravity ring; they curse because it's broken again. That makes the world real.
- **One new term at a time.** Don't introduce ten terms on the first page. Let context explain the word.

**Exposition in scene**

- **Show the effect before you explain the cause.** The reader sees that everyone wears a band around their neck long before they learn why.
- **Avoid "as you know".** Characters don't explain what both already know. Use a newcomer, a conflict or a misunderstanding instead.
- **Let the reader draw the conclusion.** Give two details and let the reader work out the third. It feels like their own discovery.
- **Short, late explanations.** A couple of sentences when the reader already wants to know, not a page when nobody has asked.

**Chapter endings**

- End on a discovery about the new thing, or on a consequence nobody saw coming.
- Even a chapter that ends quietly can leave a question about the world.

**Pacing**

- Alternate idea scenes with action. After a big discovery, the reader needs to see what it means for someone.
- Skip journeys and technical routines if nothing changes during them.
- Tag scenes with the Novum and Wonder labels. If there are long gaps between them, the idea has lost its grip on the book.
