# The novum and its consequences

The novum is the big new thing that sets the book's world apart from ours: an invention, a discovery, an event. Most of what happens in the book should follow from it.

- **One novum, not ten.** The fewer basic changes, the deeper you can go. If you add more, make sure they connect to the first.
- **Set the rules.** What can the new thing do, what can't it do, and what does it cost? Example: memories can be copied between people, but each copy fades a little compared to the original.
- **Follow the consequences three steps out.** The first step is the obvious one: memories are sold. The second is social: the poor sell their happiest days. The third is the unexpected one: courts demand witnesses' memories, and nobody knows what's real any more. The story often lives in the third step.
- **Who wins, who loses?** New technology always shifts power. Ask who owns it, who can afford it and who becomes unnecessary.
- **Everyday life changes too.** How people greet each other, what they swear about, what counts as rude. A small everyday detail convinces more than a lecture on physics.
- **Breaking the rules costs.** If something breaks the logic of the novum, it should be a turning point in the plot, never a convenient fix.
- **Show the novum in action.** The reader meets it through someone using it, struggling with it or losing because of it.

**The human question.** Every novum asks something about people: what is a self, if memories can be swapped? Make the main character's personal conflict a version of that question. Then the idea and the feeling become the same thing.

**Try this:** write down the novum, one rule, one cost and one third-step consequence. Then write a scene where that very consequence hits the main character.
