# Science and credibility

The reader forgives one big premise, but not ten small mistakes. Decide how hard your book is, and then be consistent.

- **Hard or soft.** Hard science fiction promises that the science holds, and the reader will do the maths. Soft science fiction puts the weight on society, psychology and ideas, and lets the technology be a given. Both are entirely valid, but the book must choose and stick to its choice.
- **One free premise.** The reader usually grants you one impossibility without protest: faster than light travel, time travel, the thinking machine. Everything that follows from it, however, must hold. Every further exception costs credibility.
- **Check what the reader can check.** Distances and travel times in space, the delay as signals travel between planets, what low gravity does to the body, how much energy something takes, how long food and air last. A quick estimate saves you from mistakes an informed reader never forgets.
- **Research everyday life, not just physics.** How does a hospital, a port or a factory work today? Tomorrow's institutions grow out of today's, and it is their details that bring a world to life.
- **Think about what dates.** Today's gadgets made bigger soon look old-fashioned. What lasts is human need and the patterns of power. Write technology by what it does, not by what it looks like.
- **Keep a fact list.** Put numbers and rules in your novum and place notes: length of day, travel times, population, costs. Then the same numbers apply in chapter three as in chapter thirty.
- **Confidence beats completeness.** A detail told as if it were obvious convinces more than an explanation. The person changing a filter out of habit proves more than a page on the chemistry of air purification.

**Try this:** make a rough estimate for the longest journey or the largest amount of energy in your book. Does it match what the characters experience?
