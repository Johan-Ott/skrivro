# The real people

Everyone you write about exists or has existed. Some will read the book, and some never got to choose to be in it. This is not legal advice: laws on defamation and privacy differ between countries, and a publisher or a lawyer can judge what you are unsure of. But you can make most of the hard choices yourself, in good time.

**Three questions for each person.**

- **Can they recognize themselves?** Even with another name, a profession, a place, looks and one particular event can be enough.
- **Could what I write harm them or their life?** Health, relationships, work, and the safety of children and the vulnerable.
- **Is what I tell true, and can I back it up?** What you remember is enough for a scene, but not for pointing someone out as a criminal.

**Five ways to handle it.**

- **Ask first.** The simplest and the one that saves the most relationships. Let the person read their scenes, and be ready to hear something you don't want to hear.
- **Change what isn't needed.** Change name, age, place and profession for those who need not be recognized. What makes a scene true is the feeling, not the details.
- **Merge and move, and say so.** Make one person of two or move an event in time, and write it in an author's note.
- **Cut what you can't stand behind.** If a detail isn't needed for the book's question, you don't need it. Ask: what does the book lose if it disappears?
- **Show your own part.** You are the most scrutinized person in the book. The reader trusts you more when you also show your mistakes.

**Children and the dead.** Children cannot give informed consent, and a child grows up. Write so that the child in the book can read it as an adult. The dead cannot answer, but often have family who read. The same question: can you stand behind it in front of them?

**The one who hurt you.** Sometimes the person you write about has hurt you. You have a right to your story, and the truth may be uncomfortable. Then stick to what you saw, felt and did. A theory about why they did it is a guess, and the reader can tell the difference.

**When you are unsure.** Take it to your publisher early, not after the book is printed. Go through the manuscript once with the question: who could be hurt by this page, and is the page needed? If you self-publish, have a lawyer who knows defamation read the pages you marked Sensitive.

**In Skrivro.** Mark passages with Sensitive and go through them once on their own. Make a note A person in my life for each.
