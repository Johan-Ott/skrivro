# Scene and summary

A true book moves between showing and telling. Both are needed, but in different places.

- **Scene** is a moment in real time: a place, a time, people talking and doing things. Use scene when something is at stake, when something changes, or when the reader should feel what you felt.
- **Summary** covers time quickly: "That autumn I drove to the hospital every Tuesday." Use it to link scenes, skip the waiting and give background.
- **Rule of thumb:** turning points, conflicts and goodbyes become scenes. Routines, journeys and years when nothing decisive happens become summary.
- **Build the scene on the senses.** A memory often catches on one detail: the smell of diesel, a red cardigan, the radio in the kitchen. Start there and let the rest grow out of it.
- **Dialogue you roughly remember.** Rebuild the lines as closely as you can, in the person's own way of speaking. Never put words in someone's mouth that contradict what you know of them.
- **End in motion.** Close the chapter when something has just changed, not when everything has been explained.

**A non-fiction chapter** has a shape of its own that the reader learns to recognise:

- **Open with a case** or a question that makes the problem concrete.
- **One claim per chapter,** stated plainly and early.
- **Evidence and examples** that carry the claim, with the sources logged in Groundwork.
- **What it means for the reader,** in their own everyday life.
- **A bridge** to the next chapter: the question this one leaves open.
