# The promises of memoir and non-fiction

A reader who opens a true book hands you their trust. This is what you promise in return.

**A memoir promises**

- **A chapter of a life, not a whole life.** A memoir covers a bounded stretch of time with one question at its center: the year of the illness, the move, the divorce, the years at the factory. That is what sets it apart from autobiography, which follows the whole life in order. Whatever doesn't touch the question can wait for another book. _A whole life instead becomes a chronicle without a question._
- **The truth as you remember it.** You don't invent events. You may reconstruct dialogue and detail, but not change what happened. Say so when memory is shaky: "I think it was March." _If you invent, you lose the reader's trust._
- **Openness about what you have shaped.** If you have merged several conversations into one, moved an event in time or made one person out of two, say so in a short author's note. _Readers forgive shaping, but not being fooled._
- **An honest narrator.** You are the most closely examined person in the book. Show your own mistakes as clearly as anyone else's. _A narrator with no share in the story stops being believed._
- **A transformation.** The reader wants to see how the person you were became the person you are, and what you understood along the way. Life has no plot, but the book needs one. _Without one, the reader wonders why they read it._
- **Care for real people.** The people in your book exist. Write so that you could stand behind every sentence if someone read it aloud to them. _Otherwise you can lose people, and in the worst case end up in a defamation dispute._

**A non-fiction book promises**

- **An answer to the reader's problem.** The reader picks up the book because something is unclear, hard or intriguing. The book should take them somewhere better. _If the book solves no problem, the reader puts it down halfway._
- **A clear promise, early.** By the end of the introduction the reader knows what they will be able to do, know or understand after the last page. _If the promise is vague, the reader doesn't know what they are getting._
- **A path they can follow.** Each chapter builds on the one before, and the reader always knows where they are. _If chapters don't build on each other, the reader gets lost._
- **Knowledge that holds up.** Claims are backed, and sources can be found. Where knowledge runs out, you say so. _One wrong figure makes the reader doubt the rest._
- **Real-world examples.** An idea only lands when the reader sees it at work in a case, a person or an experiment. _Without examples the idea stays abstract._

**Breaking one on purpose.** A memoir can leave the ending open if the question you asked is the one that carries. A non-fiction book can start with you if your experience is the proof, but keep it short and move quickly to the reader. Break one promise at a time and tell the reader you chose it. The guide Subgenres goes through memoir, autobiography, the essay and the others.

**Which structure fits your book?**

- **Memoir** follows a turning point: before, what happened, the reckoning and what it meant. If your life wasn't shaped by a single event, you can braid two timelines, such as your childhood and the year you are writing in, or arrange the chapters by theme. Keep the question that holds it all together.
- **Non-fiction** is built for the explanatory and the practical book: the reader should understand something or be able to do something. If you are writing narrative non-fiction, such as reportage or a work of history, borrow the memoir's tools: scenes, people and a question that pulls the reader forward.
- **Personal non-fiction,** where your own experience carries the knowledge, starts from the non-fiction structure. Use the memories as examples, not as the frame.
