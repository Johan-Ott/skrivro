# The promises of memoir and non-fiction

A reader who opens a true book hands you their trust. This is what you promise in return.

**A memoir promises**

- **A chapter of a life, not a whole life.** A memoir covers a bounded stretch of time with one question at its centre: the year of the illness, the move, the divorce, the years at the factory. That is what sets it apart from autobiography, which follows the whole life in order. Whatever doesn't touch the question can wait for another book.
- **The truth as you remember it.** You don't invent events. You may reconstruct dialogue and detail, but not change what happened. Say so when memory is shaky: "I think it was March."
- **Openness about what you have shaped.** If you have merged several conversations into one, moved an event in time or made one person out of two, say so in a short author's note. Readers forgive shaping, but not being fooled.
- **An honest narrator.** You are the most closely examined person in the book. Show your own mistakes as clearly as anyone else's.
- **A transformation.** The reader wants to see how the person you were became the person you are, and what you understood along the way. Life has no plot, but the book needs one.
- **Care for real people.** The people in your book exist. Write so that you could stand behind every sentence if someone read it aloud to them.

**A non-fiction book promises**

- **An answer to the reader's problem.** The reader picks up the book because something is unclear, hard or intriguing. The book should take them somewhere better.
- **A clear promise, early.** By the end of the introduction the reader knows what they will be able to do, know or understand after the last page.
- **A path they can follow.** Each chapter builds on the one before, and the reader always knows where they are.
- **Knowledge that holds up.** Claims are backed, and sources can be found. Where knowledge runs out, you say so.
- **Real-world examples.** An idea only lands when the reader sees it at work in a case, a person or an experiment.

**Which structure fits your book?**

- **Memoir** follows a turning point: before, what happened, the reckoning and what it meant. If your life wasn't shaped by a single event, you can braid two timelines, such as your childhood and the year you are writing in, or arrange the chapters by theme. Keep the question that holds it all together.
- **Non-fiction** is built for the explanatory and the practical book: the reader should understand something or be able to do something. If you are writing narrative non-fiction, such as reportage or a work of history, borrow the memoir's tools: scenes, people and a question that pulls the reader forward.
- **Personal non-fiction,** where your own experience carries the knowledge, starts from the non-fiction structure. Use the memories as examples, not as the frame.
