# Common pitfalls and revision

**The pitfalls**

- **Everything goes in.** A memoir that tells a whole life in order becomes a chronicle. Cut what doesn't touch the book's question, however dear it is.
- **Settling scores in print.** Readers can tell when a book exists to get even. Write about what happened and what it did to you, not about what the other person deserves.
- **A narrator with no part in it.** You don't have to take blame for what was done to you, but show what you yourself chose, believed and did.
- **The now-self lectures.** Too much interpretation smothers the scenes. Let the reader draw their own conclusions now and then.
- **Summary all the way.** Without scenes the book becomes a report. Put the most important moments in real time.
- **Non-fiction that starts with you.** Readers care about their problem first and your journey second. Place your background where it builds trust, not on page one.
- **Claims without evidence.** A single wrong figure can make the reader doubt everything else.
- **Everything at once.** A non-fiction book that tries to say everything about its subject becomes a textbook. Choose what the reader needs to solve their problem, and point onwards for the rest.

**Real people, with care**

- Write the first draft freely, as if no one would read it. Then decide, passage by passage, what goes out into the world.
- Ask of every passage: does the story need this, or do I just want to say it?
- Change names and identifying details for people who haven't chosen to be seen, and say in an author's note that you have done so. Remember that a changed name isn't enough if the job, the town and the family still point to the person.
- Take special care with children, with those who can no longer speak for themselves, and with other people's health, sexuality, finances and secrets.
- Does someone remember it differently? You don't have to give way, but you can let it show: "My brother remembers it as him making the call."
- Consider letting the person concerned read the passage before the book is done. The decision is yours, but you'll know what you are standing behind.
- If you single out someone identifiable as criminal or blameworthy, talk to the publisher or a lawyer before the book comes out. Defamation law differs between countries; in some, Sweden among them, the fact that a claim is true is not always enough on its own.

**The revision**

- Reread with one question at a time: first the book's question, then the scenes, last the language.
- Try cutting the first paragraph of each chapter. Does it start better without it?
- Check every Check facts tag against your sources in Groundwork.
- Read the dialogue aloud. Does each person sound like themselves?
- Read the book as the people in it would read it, and then as a stranger.
- Give it to a test reader who doesn't know you. Ask where they lost interest and where they stopped believing you, not what they thought of it.
